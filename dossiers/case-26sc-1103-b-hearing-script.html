03

Opening

Ninety seconds. Name the relief, name the three grounds, name the consequence. Then stop.

Stand. Wait to be recognized. Do not begin until the judge looks up.

Good morning, Your Honor. Chris Carrelha and Kelsey Carrelha, appearing pro se.

We’re here on our motion to strike the affidavit of Trenna Farr, dated February 10, 2026. We have three grounds, and I’ll take them in that order.

First, the affidavit does not show personal knowledge, which O.C.G.A. section 9-11-56(e) requires on the face of the affidavit itself.

Second, the records it relies on do not qualify under the business records exception, because the circumstances of their preparation indicate a lack of trustworthiness — and section 24-8-803(6) makes that an express limit on the exception, not an afterthought.

Third, the affidavit contradicts what Regions has already said in writing about this same account.

If the affidavit is stricken, Your Honor, there is no admissible evidence before the Court supporting the balance Regions claims.

If the judge interrupts to ask which ground is strongest: say Pillar I — personal knowledge. It is decided on the four corners of the affidavit, it needs no witness, and it does not require the Court to resolve any factual dispute. Then offer the other two as independently sufficient.

04

Pillar I — Personal knowledge § 9-11-56(e)

The four-corners argument. This is the ground you can win without a witness, without an exhibit, and without the Court weighing anything.

The rule

Your Honor, section 9-11-56(e) requires three things, and it requires them to appear on the face of the affidavit: that it be made on personal knowledge; that it set forth facts that would be admissible in evidence; and that it show affirmatively that the affiant is competent to testify to the matters stated.

That last requirement is the one that matters here. It is not enough that Ms. Farr is an employee of Regions. The affidavit has to show, affirmatively, that she knows what she is swearing to.

The application

Fill from the affidavit Quote the affidavit’s personal-knowledge recital verbatim and give the paragraph number. It is almost always a formula along the lines of “I am over 18, I am employed by Regions Bank, and I have personal knowledge of the matters stated herein based on my review of the business records.” The phrase “based on my review of the records” is the target — it is a concession that the knowledge is derived, not personal. Write the exact words and the paragraph number here before the hearing.

What the affidavit actually says is that Ms. Farr’s knowledge comes from her review of the file. Your Honor, that is a description of reading, not of knowing.

She does not say she made any of these entries. She does not say she supervised anyone who made them. She does not say when she first looked at this account, or what she did to satisfy herself that what she was reading was accurate.

A records custodian can authenticate a document — can say, this came out of our system, this is what our system holds. That is a real and limited function. What a custodian cannot do is swear to the truth of what the document asserts, unless she has personal knowledge of the underlying events. This affidavit never establishes that she does.

Hold the distinction. Authentication versus substantive truth is the hinge of the whole pillar. Opposing counsel will conflate them — will argue that because Farr can authenticate, she can swear to the balance. She cannot. Say the sentence “authentication is not the same as personal knowledge of the facts asserted” as many times as it takes.

The relief on this pillar

On this ground alone, Your Honor, the affidavit should be stricken — and the Court doesn’t need to reach the records or the contradiction to get there. It’s decided on the four corners of the affidavit.

05

Pillar II — Trustworthiness § 24-8-803(6)

Every fact in this passage is drawn from the filed Answer’s Fifth and Eighth Defenses. It is all safe to say.

The rule — read the last clause

Your Honor, the business records exception is not automatic, and the last clause of section 24-8-803(6) is the whole clause. The record comes in unless the source of the information, or the method or circumstances of preparation, indicate a lack of trustworthiness.

Here they do indicate exactly that. And the burden of laying that foundation is on Regions. It is not our burden to disprove it.

The three marks of a rewritten record

Three things on the face of Regions’ own record.

First. In September of 2023 we tendered payments totaling approximately twenty-nine thousand nine hundred forty dollars. Regions accepted those payments and posted them to the account. Then it reversed them — unilaterally, without notice and without our consent. Its ledger carries reversal entries dated September 18, 2023.

Second. On October 23, 2025 — two years later, and after this dispute was underway — Regions booked an entry it labeled an “Interest Credit Adjustment,” carrying the comment “adjustment related to core conversion.”

Third. The account carries NSF fee entries that were inserted retroactively.

Your Honor, those are not the marks of a record made at or near the time of the event, in the regular course of business, by someone with knowledge. Those are the marks of a record that was reconstructed after the fact, during a system migration, while this dispute was live.

Once the method of preparation is itself in question, the exception does not apply. And Ms. Farr’s affidavit does not address a single one of these entries.

The strongest single sentence in the case is the core-conversion comment. It is Regions’ own words, in Regions’ own system, describing a data migration as the reason for a money entry on a disputed account. If you get one sentence out before the judge cuts you off, make it that one.

Fill from the ledger Give the Court the exact page or Bates number for each of the three entry groups — the Sept. 18, 2023 reversals, the Oct. 23, 2025 adjustment, and the retroactive NSF entries — keyed to the binder tab. Do not describe an entry you cannot point to on a page you have handed up.
06

Pillar III — The contradiction

The CFPB admission is confirmed. The Prophecy framing needs care — read the strategy note before you commit to it at the podium.

The admission

Hand up Tab 04. Wait until the Court has it.

Your Honor, at Tab 4 is Regions’ own written response to our complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, case number 260101-27348878.

In that response, Regions states that the twenty-thousand-dollar payment was split into separate amounts.

That is Regions’ document, Your Honor. It is not our characterization and it is not argument — it is what the bank wrote when it was answering to its regulator.

Fill from the Farr affidavit Identify the paragraph of the affidavit that describes the handling of that payment, and quote it beside the CFPB language so the Court can hold the two side by side. The contrast has to be exact. If, after reading both, the two statements can be reconciled, say so and drop this pillar — do not stretch it. Pillars I and II stand on their own.

The doctrine

Under Prophecy Corp. v. Rossignol, when a party offers testimony that is self-contradictory on a material point and offers no reasonable explanation for the contradiction, the contradictory testimony is construed against that party and is not considered.

Regions has offered no explanation. We would ask the Court to construe the conflict against Regions.

Know the weakness before opposing counsel finds it. Prophecy is classically applied to the self-contradictory testimony of a party on summary judgment — the same person saying two different things under oath. Here the two statements come from different places: a sworn affidavit by a bank employee, and an unsworn corporate letter to a regulator. Expect Regions to argue Prophecy doesn’t reach that, and expect the argument to have some force.

The fallback is better than the doctrine, so keep it in your pocket: the contradiction is a trustworthiness fact. Whether or not Prophecy applies by its own terms, a record that says one thing to a regulator and another to a court is a record whose “circumstances of preparation indicate lack of trustworthiness” under § 24-8-803(6). If the judge pushes back on Prophecy, say:

Your Honor, if the Court reads Prophecy more narrowly than that, the conflict still matters — it goes to trustworthiness under 803(6). A record that tells the regulator one thing and this Court another is a record whose circumstances of preparation indicate a lack of trustworthiness on their face.

07

If Farr testifies — cross-examination

Only live if she is subpoenaed and appears. Short, closed questions. Each one is built to lock a single admission — ask it, get the answer, move on. Do not ask a question whose answer you do not already know.

Sequence

  1. Establish the date of first contact

    “Ms. Farr, before February 10, 2026 — the day you signed this affidavit — had you ever personally worked on this account?”

    Either answer helps. “No” ends Pillar I. “Yes” opens the question of what she did, when, and why none of it is in the affidavit.

  2. Separate her from the entries

    “Did you personally make any of the entries on the transaction history attached to your affidavit?”

    Locks in that her knowledge of the record’s contents is derived from reading it.

  3. Separate her from the people who did

    “Do you know the name of any person who made any of those entries?”

    Confirms she cannot speak to the source of the information — the first half of the 803(6) trustworthiness clause.

  4. Open the migration

    “Was this account moved from one servicing system to another?”

    Sets up the core-conversion entry without tipping where you’re going.

  5. Close the migration

    “Can you tell this Court which entries on that history were created before the migration and which were created by it?”

    The pillar-II admission. If she cannot, the record’s method of preparation is unestablished — by Regions’ own witness.

  6. Set the CFPB conflict

    “Are you familiar with the written response Regions sent to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in case number 260101-27348878?”

    “No” establishes she is not the person with knowledge of the account’s history. “Yes” sets up the next question.

  7. Spring it — only if she said yes

    “Regions told the Bureau that the twenty-thousand-dollar payment was split into separate amounts. Is that correct?”

    The contradiction, from the witness stand, in her voice rather than yours.

Discipline

Never ask why. Not once. “Why” hands her the floor and invites the explanation that cures the Prophecy problem. Every question above is answerable yes, no, or “I don’t know” — and “I don’t know” is a winning answer for you on every one of them.

Stop when you have it. The temptation after question 5 is to keep going and drive it home. Don’t. You have the admission; the argument is yours to make in closing, not hers to rebut on the stand.

If she volunteers narrative, let her finish, then: “Ms. Farr, my question was whether you made the entries. Did you?” Repeat the question, unchanged, as many times as needed. The Court will notice.

If opposing counsel objects to form, rephrase into a shorter closed question rather than arguing the objection. You lose nothing and you keep the rhythm.

If she is not present, say so plainly and once: “Your Honor, Regions offers this affidavit but has not produced the affiant. We’ve had no opportunity to test any of it.” Then return to the four corners.

Fill from the affidavit Add follow-up questions tied to specific paragraph numbers once you have read it. Every question you add must rest on a document already in the binder.
08

Rebuttal — distinguishing Windham

Expect Regions to cite Windham v. State, 344 Ga. App. 721, for the proposition that a custodian need not have made the entries personally. Confirm the citation and holding yourself before the hearing.

Your Honor, Windham doesn’t answer the question before the Court, for two reasons.

First, the posture. Windham is a criminal case, in which records were admitted at trial after a live witness laid the foundation and was subject to cross-examination. What’s before this Court is a paper affidavit, offered without the affiant, on a motion. Section 9-11-56(e) is a summary judgment standard, and it imposes a requirement that no criminal evidentiary ruling addresses.

Second, the issue. We’re not arguing that a custodian must personally have made every entry. We agree she need not. We’re arguing something narrower: that this affidavit doesn’t satisfy 9-11-56(e)’s affirmative showing, and that these records carry circumstances of preparation that defeat the exception under the last clause of 803(6). Windham speaks to neither.

Concede the point they’re actually right about. Giving up “a custodian need not have made the entries” costs you nothing — it was never your argument — and it buys credibility for the narrower ground that wins.

The ask

Land this word for word. Primary relief, alternative relief, consequence, sit down.

Your Honor, we ask three things.

First, that the Court strike the affidavit of Trenna Farr in its entirety.

In the alternative, that the Court strike the paragraphs identified in our motion and the exhibits attached to them.

And because Regions’ motion rests on that affidavit, that the motion be denied.

We have a proposed order, Your Honor. Thank you.

Hand up the proposed order only if invited. Then sit.

Contingency scripts

Two passages for the moments that go wrong. Learn both cold; you will not have time to read.

If the Court signals it will deny — the fifteen-second rescue

Your Honor, before the Court rules — may I have fifteen seconds?

The one fact I’d ask the Court to hold onto is this. Regions’ own written response to the CFPB says the payment was split into separate amounts. The affidavit says otherwise. That’s not our argument — it’s their document, and it’s at Tab 4.

If the Court is inclined to deny, we would ask that it be denied without prejudice, so the conflict can be taken up on a developed record. Thank you, Your Honor.

If opposing counsel argues facts not in evidence — Rankin objection

Objection, Your Honor. Counsel is testifying.

What he’s describing isn’t in the affidavit and isn’t in any exhibit before the Court. If Regions wants that fact in evidence, it needs a witness. We’d ask that the statement be disregarded.

On Rankin v. Smith: the principle — that counsel’s statements are not evidence and an advocate may not supply facts from the podium — is well settled and you can argue it without a citation. Confirm the reporter citation before naming the case aloud. If you cannot verify it in time, make the objection on the principle alone. An unverified cite read to the Court costs you more than no cite at all.

If the Court takes the motion under advisement: “Thank you, Your Honor. Would the Court like anything further from us in writing?” Then stop. Do not re-argue.
