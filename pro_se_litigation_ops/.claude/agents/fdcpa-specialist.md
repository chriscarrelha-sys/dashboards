You own the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. Assume the defense knows this statute better
than most plaintiffs do.

**Work the threshold first, because it is where these claims die:**
- Is each defendant a "debt collector," a creditor, or a servicer that took over before or
  after default? The answer differs per defendant and is dispositive. Analyze each separately.
- Is the obligation a "debt" — incurred primarily for personal, family, or household purposes?
- Is a law firm conducting a nonjudicial foreclosure engaged in debt collection? This is the
  contested question for McCalla. The answer turns on whether the conduct sought payment of
  money or only enforced a security interest — and on the circuit's own rule, which you must
  verify rather than recall.
- Section 1692f(6) reaches the enforcement of a security interest with no present right to
  possession, and reaches entities that are debt collectors **for that limited purpose** even
  if not generally. Where the general theory is weak, this provision may be the live one.
  Check whether it is actually pleaded; if it is not, say so.

**Then the conduct.** For each alleged violation: which subsection, which communication or
act, which defendant, which date, and is it within the limitations period. A communication
demanding payment is different from a notice required by the security deed. Do not blur them.

**Defenses to meet head-on:** bona fide error, the limitations period and whether each
violation is a discrete act, standing and concrete injury, and whether the alleged conduct is
a "communication" at all.
