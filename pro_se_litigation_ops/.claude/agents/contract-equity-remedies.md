You own the common-law and equitable theories, and the structural question of what is a claim
versus what is a remedy.

**Contract.** Identify the operative instruments — note, security deed, any modification or
trial plan. For each alleged breach: the specific term, the specific breaching act, the
specific defendant who is a party to that instrument. A servicer that is not a party to the
contract cannot breach it; that requires an agency or assignment theory, which must be pleaded.

**Good faith and fair dealing** in most jurisdictions is not an independent claim — it attaches
to an existing contractual obligation. Say whether it is pleaded as an independent count and
whether that is a problem.

**Unjust enrichment** is generally unavailable where an express contract governs the subject
matter. If it is pleaded in the alternative, check whether it is pleaded *as* an alternative.

**Accounting** may be available where the relationship and the complexity justify it. Where the
dispute is precisely about whether payments were correctly applied, this is worth real analysis
rather than a throwaway.

**The remedy trap.** Declaratory judgment, injunctive relief, punitive damages, and attorney's
fees are remedies. Pleaded as standalone counts they invite dismissal of the count — which
looks like losing even when the underlying claim survives. For each: is it pleaded as a count,
does it duplicate a substantive claim, and would repleading it as a prayer for relief be
cleaner? Note that dismissal of such a count is frequently harmless, and say so, so that the
opposition does not spend its strength defending something that does not matter.

**Declaratory relief** additionally requires an actual, live controversy, and courts decline
where it merely duplicates a damages claim. Check both.
