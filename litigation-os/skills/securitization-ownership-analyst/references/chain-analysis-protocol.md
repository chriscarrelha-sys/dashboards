# Chain Analysis Protocol

## Why this area produces bad arguments

Securitization analysis attracts theories that are internally coherent and
legally dead. They persist because the documents genuinely are complicated, the
inconsistencies genuinely exist, and the leap from "this is irregular" to "this is
unenforceable" feels small. It is not small. It is the whole case.

The discipline: **establish the facts precisely, then ask a research specialist
what the forum does with them.** Never supply the legal answer yourself.

## The parties in a typical securitization

Understanding the normal structure is what lets you tell an anomaly from an
ordinary feature.

| Party | Function |
|---|---|
| **Originator** | Makes the loan |
| **Warehouse lender** | Funds origination short-term, often with a security interest in the loan until sold |
| **Sponsor / seller** | Aggregates loans and initiates the securitization |
| **Depositor** | Special-purpose entity; buys from sponsor, conveys to the trust (the step that creates bankruptcy remoteness) |
| **Issuing trust** | Holds the loans, issues the notes or certificates |
| **Trustee** | Administers the trust for noteholders |
| **Custodian** | Physically holds notes and loan files; often the only party who can say who has the original |
| **Master servicer / servicer / subservicer** | Collect, apply, report, and pursue default remedies |
| **Nominee (registry)** | Holds legal title to the security instrument as nominee, tracking transfers electronically |

A chain touching six entities is normal. Complexity is not a defect.

## The five concepts, and why conflating them is fatal

Written out again because it is the mistake that matters most:

1. **Ownership of the debt** — the economic interest.
2. **Holder / right to enforce** — possession plus endorsement, or bearer status.
3. **Security-interest assignment** — who holds the recorded lien.
4. **Servicing authority** — who may collect and act.
5. **Investor reporting** — whom the servicer reports to.

Different entities occupy these simultaneously, lawfully. Before calling two
sources inconsistent, identify which of the five each one speaks to. A registry
lookup naming an "investor" and a letter naming an "owner" may be answering
different questions with different vocabularies — or may be a genuine conflict.
Determining which is the analysis.

## The eight date types

Capture all eight; compare them deliberately.

Ordering patterns worth recording, each with its innocent explanation:

| Pattern | Innocent explanation |
|---|---|
| Assignment executed long after the economic transfer | Assignments are routinely prepared only when needed, often at default. Ordinary. |
| Recording long after execution | Recording lag is normal and, in most states, does not affect validity between the parties. |
| Transfer into a trust after its cut-off date | May breach the trust's own agreement — but the borrower is generally not a party to that agreement, which is where most such arguments fail. |
| Notarization dated before execution | Usually a clerical error; occasionally more. A narrow, factual point. |
| Assignment by an entity that had already assigned | Could be a correction of a defective earlier instrument; could be error. |
| Undated endorsement | Extremely common and generally unproblematic. |

Record the pattern **and** the explanation. A chronology listing anomalies without
their ordinary explanations is an advocacy document that will not survive review.

## MERS and nominee structures

A registry nominee typically holds legal title to the security instrument as
nominee for the lender and its successors, while beneficial ownership moves
electronically. Two consequences:

- The absence of recorded assignments between successive owners is **expected**,
  not suspicious. That is what the structure is for.
- A registry's public lookup is a **servicing-system record**, not a title record.
  It can lag, it can be wrong, and it is populated by the servicer. Treat it as a
  party statement about what that party believes — useful precisely because it is
  the defendant's own data — not as proof of ownership.

Where a registry lookup names a different investor than a recorded assignment or a
servicer letter, the finding is: **the defendants' own records are internally
inconsistent about who owns this loan.** That is a records-reliability point and a
discovery target. It is usually not a title defect, and framing it as one gives
the other side an easy answer.

## Notes, endorsements, allonges

- **Endorsement in blank** makes the note payable to bearer; whoever possesses it
  may generally enforce it.
- **Special endorsement** names the party who may enforce.
- **Allonge** — a paper affixed to the note carrying further endorsements, used
  when the note has no room. Questions: is it firmly affixed, and does it identify
  the note it belongs to? Both are narrow factual questions and both are
  occasionally winnable.
- **Possession** ordinarily matters more than the recorded chain for enforcing the
  note. Where the note is endorsed in blank, a long chain of unrecorded transfers
  may be legally irrelevant.

When the note is not in your sources: "no copy of the note appears among the
sources reviewed." Never "the note is missing." You have seen a subset of a file.

## Commonly rejected theories

State each in the result's rejected list with its one-line refutation, and flag
that the forum-specific rule must come from research.

| Theory | Why it generally fails |
|---|---|
| "Securitization split the note from the deed, voiding the lien" | Courts overwhelmingly reject the split-the-note theory; the security follows the debt. |
| "The trust violated its own agreement, so the transfer is void" | The borrower is not a party to that agreement and generally lacks standing; many courts also hold such a breach voidable, not void. |
| "A defect in the assignment means nobody can foreclose" | The obligation survives; at most a different party enforces. |
| "Show me the note as a freestanding demand" | Not a cause of action in most jurisdictions. |
| "No recorded assignment appears, so no transfer occurred" | Registry structures are designed to avoid recording each transfer. |
| "The loan was paid off by insurance or credit enhancement" | Third-party recoveries generally do not discharge the borrower. |
| "Robo-signing voids the instrument" | Signature-authority irregularities rarely void an instrument, and the borrower usually lacks standing. |

Note the difference between these and a **live** question: whether the party that
actually sent a default notice or scheduled a sale had authority at that moment
is a real issue in many states, and is not on this list.

## What tends to be genuinely material

- Whether the entity that exercised a power of sale held it **at that time**.
- Facial defects in the instrument relied on for that power.
- Notices sent by an entity with no authority to send them.
- **Defendants' own inconsistent statements** about ownership — valuable as
  evidence of unreliable records and as a basis for discovery, and available
  without any standing problem because you are not challenging the assignment,
  you are pointing at their file.
- Whether the obligation itself is enforceable — a question about the loan, not
  about the securitization, and one that avoids the standing bar entirely.

The last point is the important strategic one. Theories attacking the *chain*
mostly fail on standing. Theories attacking the *underlying obligation* — failure
of consideration, funding that never occurred — belong to the borrower and do not
raise the standing problem at all. Where the evidence supports it, that is the
stronger road.

## Recording what you could not see

Every chain analysis ends with an explicit list of what was not available: the
note, the allonge, the servicing agreement, the trust's governing documents, the
mortgage loan schedule, the custodian's records, the purchase agreements. For each,
say who would have it. That list is the securitization analyst's most reliably
useful output, because it converts directly into subpoenas.
