# Instrument Selection and Drafting

## The sequencing logic

Discovery has an order that repays following:

1. **Preserve first.** A litigation-hold letter costs nothing and converts later
   deletion into spoliation. It is a communication, so it requires authorization.
2. **Public records and the client's own files before anything is served.** Deeds,
   recorded assignments, published notices, agency files, the client's bank
   records — obtainable without discovery, faster, and free of objection. A
   request for something you could have pulled yourself invites a proportionality
   objection that colours everything around it.
3. **Documents before testimony.** Deposing a witness without their documents
   wastes the deposition and usually means asking for a second one.
4. **Interrogatories concurrently with document requests**, aimed at what
   documents cannot answer — identification, computation, contentions.
5. **Third-party subpoenas early**, because non-parties are slow and their
   responses often reshape what you ask the parties.
6. **Requests for admission late**, once you know what is genuinely disputed.
7. **Depositions last**, with a complete document set.

## Document requests

**Good:** bounded by date, defined by category, tied to an identifiable
transaction, account, or communication.

> "All documents concerning the application of the payment of $10,000.00
> received on or about September 15, 2023 to account number [X], including
> screenshots, system notes, internal correspondence, and any document reflecting
> the reason for the entry dated September 26, 2023."

**Bad:** unbounded in time, category-free, or a demand for "all documents
relating to" a broad concept. It draws an overbreadth objection and a motion you
did not need.

Always specify **form of production** for ESI, and ask for metadata where the
timing of a record matters.

Ask for **the system's own documentation** as well as its output: code keys,
procedure manuals, retention policies. These are frequently more probative than
the records themselves and are rarely produced unless asked for by name.

## Interrogatories

Numerically limited, so spend them where only a sworn answer will do:

- **Identification.** Who made a decision, who has knowledge, who holds a record.
- **Computation.** "State how the figure of $X in the payoff quote dated D was
  calculated, identifying each component." Extremely effective where a total
  appears with no breakdown.
- **Contention.** "State each fact supporting the contention that the account was
  due for the January 1, 2024 payment." Forces commitment to a position.
- **Chain.** "Identify each entity that has held an interest in the Note from
  origination to the present, and the date and instrument of each transfer."

Never spend an interrogatory asking for documents.

## Requests for admission

Two distinct uses:

**Narrowing.** "Admit that no payment in the amount of $5,200.00 was received on
the account between December 1 and December 31, 2023." Either answer helps: an
admission resolves it; a denial tells you their position and sets up impeachment
if the records say otherwise.

**Authentication.** "Admit that the document attached as Exhibit A is a true and
correct copy of a business record of Defendant." Cheap, and it removes a real
trial problem.

Draft each RFA so that it addresses **one fact**. A compound RFA gets a qualified
denial and wastes the request.

## Rule 30(b)(6) topics

The instrument for binding an organisation on how its own systems work.

**Good topic:** "The meaning of each transaction code, reversal code, and flag
appearing on the account history for account [X], including the circumstances in
which each is applied."

**Bad topic:** "The account." Unbounded topics draw objections and produce an
unprepared witness, which is its own problem — though an unprepared witness on a
properly noticed topic is a sanctionable failure to prepare, and sometimes that
is the more useful outcome.

Topics that repeatedly matter in servicing disputes: the application waterfall;
suspense and unapplied funds policy; what triggers an NSF entry and whether one
can be recorded without an actual return; how a loan is boarded on a servicing
transfer and what data carries over; the fee schedule and the authority for each
fee; the loss-mitigation review process and what "incomplete" means; how investor
and owner information is recorded and updated.

## Individual depositions

Depose the person who **signed** something, **decided** something, or
**perceived** something. A letter's signatory is a better witness than a
corporate designee about why that letter said what it said.

For each deponent record: what they can testify to from their own knowledge, the
exhibits, the three answers you need, and what you will do if the answer is
unhelpful.

## Third-party subpoenas

Non-parties hold much of what matters in a servicing case: the borrower's own
bank (whether payments cleared and were not returned), the closing attorney and
title agent (where origination funds went), the prior servicer if no longer a
party, the trustee or custodian (who holds the note), consumer reporting agencies
(what was furnished and when), and a prospective employer (whether a report was
pulled and what it showed).

Third-party discovery has its own notice requirements and is slower. Start early.

## Drafting patterns that survive objection

- **Define terms once**, in a definitions section, and use them consistently.
- **Bound every request by date**, tied to something in the record.
- **Prefer a series of narrow requests** to one broad one — a partial objection
  then kills one request rather than all of them.
- **Ask for the absence too**: "or, if no such document exists, so state." This
  turns silence into a usable answer.
- **Ask how records are kept** before fighting about what they contain.

## Testing your own draft

Before a request enters the roadmap, ask:

1. Which gap does this close? If none, cut it.
2. Which element does it serve? If none, cut it.
3. Does this party have it, and why do I believe that?
4. What is the strongest objection, and have I narrowed to meet it?
5. What do I learn if they refuse?
6. What do I do with it when it arrives?

A request that cannot answer all six is not ready.
